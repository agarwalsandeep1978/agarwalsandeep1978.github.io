Maker Profile · Project 04

Therapeutic Hoodie

I designed and stitched a therapeutic hoodie prototype for children with ADHD, exploring whether adjustable pressure and gentle weight could make everyday clothing more supportive without looking clinical or drawing unwanted attention.

The design need

My research focused on children aged approximately 5–9 and the challenges some experience with attention, anxiety and sensory regulation. I wanted to explore how apparel could provide physical comfort while remaining familiar, wearable and appropriate for school. The design brief asked me to consider the needs of children as well as the practical concerns of parents and caregivers, including comfort, durability and ease of use.

Designing and making

I developed a hoodie with adjustable nylon and Velcro straps, buckles, elasticated sections and weighted inserts. I planned the garment by sketching and sizing the main hoodie components—including the body, sleeves, hood and pocket—and separately mapping the therapeutic accessories. I then sourced fabric and the supporting materials, cut the components and assembled the garment. The adjustable straps were intended to let the wearer change the fit and pressure, while the weighted inserts added gentle weight without making the garment excessively heavy.

Material selection and construction involved trade-offs. The straps needed to be adjustable but secure; the garment needed to be comfortable while accommodating extra components; and the seams and accessories could not make the hoodie irritating or difficult to maintain. I considered adding electronic feedback, including Wi-Fi-enabled features, but did not incorporate electronics into the final garment because safety, washability and further testing would need more development.

Evaluation and learning

The project showed me how quickly a promising concept becomes a series of practical design decisions. I would continue improving the adjustability of the weighted inserts, simplify maintenance and test comfort and fit more systematically with the intended users and appropriate adult guidance. The experience taught me to treat accessibility, safety and dignity as core design requirements—not as additions after the prototype is built.
